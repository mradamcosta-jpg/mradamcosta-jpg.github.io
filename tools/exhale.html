How it works — the baroreflex, in plain language

Your body has a built-in cruise control for blood pressure called the baroreflex. Think of the thermostat on a wall: sensors in the walls of your big arteries constantly read the pressure and nudge your heart rate and vessel tightness up or down to hold a set point, second by second.

Here is the useful quirk. Your blood pressure isn't flat — it rises a touch when you breathe in and eases when you breathe out, like a swing rocking back and forth. When you slow your breathing to about six breaths a minute, that swing lines up with the body's own slow pressure wave (physiologists call it the Mayer wave). It's the difference between shoving a playground swing at random moments versus pushing exactly in time — push in rhythm and the same effort moves the swing far more. Breathing in that rhythm gives the baroreflex a bigger, cleaner signal to work with, and over weeks the reflex gets more sensitive and better at keeping pressure down.

The long exhale does a second job. Breathing out slowly leans on the "rest and digest" side of your nervous system — the brake pedal, as opposed to the fight-or-flight accelerator. That dials down the background nervous-system "tone" that keeps vessels tight, which is a big part of what sets resting pressure. Same reason a long sigh makes you feel your shoulders drop.

What the evidence says

Slow breathing is one of the better-studied non-drug ways to nudge blood pressure down. It's a gentle lever, not a hammer — useful for pre-high or mildly high pressure and as a companion to the bigger levers (less salt, less alcohol, isometric holds), not a replacement for medication in serious cases.

The mechanism — six breaths a minute sharpening baroreflex sensitivity — is well established in the lab, including in people with high blood pressure and heart failure. The honest caveat: the trials are short (the longest run about nine weeks), so the very-long-term payoff isn't proven. This timer does the same job those studies used a dedicated gadget for — it just paces your breath.

How much, and what to expect

The dose the research is built on is simple and small: about 15 minutes a day, most days, at roughly six breaths a minute. That's where the numbers above come from — it isn't a "more is always better" situation, so there's no need to grind out an hour. Fifteen focused minutes is the target.
